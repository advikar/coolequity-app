From a map to a defensible shortlist

Find where canopy investment deserves a closer look, then explore the scale of a street-tree intervention.

Use it to

Turn a citywide canopy and heat map into a focused conversation about where to invest. A city team can shortlist areas for field assessment, compare policy priorities, and estimate the scale of a street-tree program in one place.

Why this approach

We bring canopy, residents and home-cooling context onto the same grid so staff can compare areas without assembling separate maps. The workflow connects a priority to its evidence and a planting scenario.

Source & calculation details
1. Choose a ranked area. 2. Read its metrics. 3. Adjust the planting share. Use Explore to compare layers or change ranking weights. The map, list and score breakdown update together.

Planning note Use the shortlist to scope an assessment; confirm site feasibility and local costs before committing funds.

Priority score & ranking

A relative 0–100 index of where canopy investment may be most needed in this city.

Use it to

Build a first-round investment shortlist that accounts for both missing shade and the people living with it. Open any area to explain the drivers behind its position in a budget or program-scoping discussion.

Why this approach

An additive score makes each input’s contribution inspectable. Surface heat carries the largest default share because Bakersfield is one flat valley climate, so a hotter cell is a hotter place to live rather than a different climate; missing canopy, A/C and age add resident context. Population adjusts potential reach. These are explicit project policy choices, not externally prescribed weights.

Source & calculation details
Defaults: 45% surface heat + 35% missing canopy + 20% age 65+; A/C carries no default weight because Census LACE puts it at 96.5–100% of homes across ranked cells. Inputs are scaled to 0–1; protective inputs enter as 1 − scaled value. Multiply the sum by (0.55 + 0.45 × scaled population), then rescale residential results to 0–100. The pipeline clips the low-need tail: heat and age at p2, canopy and A/C at p98; population saturates at p98. Exact endpoints ship in the GeoJSON norm object.

Planning note Scores compare residential cells within this city; they are not health-risk probabilities or cross-city rankings.

Rank stability A rank is an estimate with a band, not an exact position. 05b_stability.py re-draws each block group’s population and residents 65+ and each tract’s A/C estimate inside their published 90% margins of error (neighbouring areas cut from the same block group move together), jitters each non-zero recommended weight by ±10 points, and re-ranks; 300 draws. The detail panel shows each area’s 5th–95th percentile rank and how often it lands in the top tier (best 199 of 1,987). In this build the median band is 279 ranks wide, and of the 199 top-tier areas 51% stay top-tier in at least 80% of draws. Surface temperature and tree cover are held fixed (measurement error, no per-area margin published), as is the allocation of residents within a block group. Treat rank differences smaller than an area’s band as not established (the bands are sensitivity ranges under stated assumptions, not confidence intervals or a pairwise test); use the top-tier share to shortlist.

Presets & custom weights

Ask a different policy question and see the shortlist change.

Use it to

Test whether a proposed focus area remains a priority when staff emphasize shade, older residents or access to cooling. Use presets to structure a policy discussion instead of treating one ranking as the only answer.

Why this approach

Editable weights expose the value judgments behind a composite index. The JRC’s guidance supports examining rankings under alternative assumptions; it does not endorse our particular presets. Normalizing weights keeps their relative contribution explicit.

Source & calculation details
Recommended mix is the shipped model. Fewest trees isolates tree cover, still adjusted for population. Heat first leans on surface heat. Older residents emphasizes age; Homes without A/C emphasizes A/C; Far from cooling adds walking time. Most people raises the population multiplier. Input sliders are normalized to sum to 100%; the population slider changes the separate multiplier. Reset restores the defaults.

Count people more (the population multiplier)

The four condition sliders say what counts; this slider says how much the number of residents counts. Each area first gets a need figure from its conditions. That figure is then multiplied by a factor that runs from (1 − P) for the least-populated area up to 1 for the most-populated, where P is the slider. At P = 0% every area is judged on conditions alone. At the recommended 45%, the most-populated area counts about 1.8× the least. At 100%, an area with almost no residents scores near zero whatever its heat or shade, because there is almost nobody there to help. Population is normalized between its minimum and the 98th percentile of ranked areas (risk inputs run from their 2nd percentile to their maximum; protective inputs from their minimum to their 98th percentile), so one very crowded area does not flatten everyone else.

Worked example
Two areas with the same conditions score 0.60 before population. One holds 40 residents (near the bottom of the range), the other 620 (near the top). At P = 45% the first is multiplied by about 0.58 and the second by about 1.0, so they score 0.35 and 0.60: the populous area ranks well ahead. At P = 0% both stay at 0.60 and tie. At P = 90% the small one falls to 0.09.

Planning note Presets represent alternative priorities. They change rankings, not planting costs.

Surface heat

Satellite surface temperature, not the air temperature people breathe.

Use it to

See the outdoor thermal context around proposed investments and compare it with canopy gaps. The separate heat view helps staff distinguish a shade-priority question from a surface-heat question.

Why this approach

Landsat provides a repeatable, spatially consistent thermal record. Summer medians reduce dependence on a single scene. Heat carries the largest default weight here because Bakersfield is one flat valley floor with no marine gradient, so within-city surface-temperature comparisons compare like with like. Contra Costa, by contrast, leaves heat unscored.

Source & calculation details
Landsat 8/9 Collection 2 surface-temperature band ST_B10, via Planetary Computer; medians of 104 summer scenes (June 1–September 15, under 20% cloud) from 2024, 2025 and 2026, the three most recent summers. Builds before November 11, 2026 used 2022–2024; moving the window changed every surface-temperature value. Mid-morning overpasses sample particular clear-sky days, not the hottest afternoon or a continuous record.

Planning note Surface temperature is not air temperature or a live heat alert. Current scenes cover summers 2024–2026.

Tree canopy

The share of assessed ground covered by tree crowns.

Use it to

Locate shade deficits and establish a baseline for scoping a canopy assessment. Staff can compare canopy coverage across candidate areas before choosing where to investigate street-tree opportunities.

Why this approach

The USFS/CAL FIRE aerial canopy classification targets tree crowns, which fits a shade-investment question more directly than general greenness. Equal-area aggregation preserves ground-area proportions; the assessed urban boundary defines the denominator. Validation. The USFS map stops at the 2020 Census urban-area line, and 213 ranked areas of newer housing lie past it. There, tree cover is modelled from the same June 2022 aerial survey (NAIP) the USFS map was classified from, by a classifier trained on the USFS map and scored on areas it never saw. Against the USFS figure, in percentage points of tree cover, the model is off by 0.9 on average in urban areas, 0.6 on open land and 1.2 on areas the line cuts through, with no bias over 0.12; the canopy-height model this replaces was off by 1.8 and 3.2. Bakersfield is outside the Bay Area lidar vegetation map used to validate the Contra Costa builds, so the USFS map itself has not been checked against lidar here; USGS 3DEP lidar or field checks are the route to that.

Source & calculation details
Primary layer: USFS/CAL FIRE California Urban Tree Canopy, 2022 NAIP aerial classification at 0.6 m native resolution. The pipeline aggregates canopy pixels in equal-area coordinates and clips denominators to assessed urban boundaries. Past the assessed urban boundary, and in the 63 cells where that boundary leaves under 10% of the cell assessed, pipeline step 02g models tree cover for the whole cell from NAIP 2022 (60 cm; red, green, blue, near-infrared; flown June 7–8, 2022): a gradient-boosted per-pixel classifier on 18 colour and texture features, trained on 2.2 million pixels from 524 cells with the USFS map as the label, and a cell’s cover is the mean predicted probability over its pixels. Training and testing use three kinds of cell, because land past the urban line is not like land inside it: urban (300 trained, 300 held out), open land that ESA WorldCover calls cropland, grassland, bare ground, shrub or woodland (101 and 101), and cells the boundary cuts, using only their assessed part (123 and 123). Held-out results against the USFS figure (bias, mean absolute error, rank correlation): urban −0.12, 0.92, 0.963; open land −0.01, 0.62, 0.974; edge +0.08, 1.18, 0.963. The canopy-height model on the same cells: urban 1.75 mean absolute error (1.51 after rescaling), rank correlation 0.924; open land 3.24, 0.861. The stage refuses to write anything unless it beats the height model on urban and on open-land cells. A first version trained on urban cells alone scored 1.0 on urban cells and then read irrigated alfalfa and pasture as tree canopy (65% on a field of row crops); aerial photos of the worst cells showed it, which is why open land is trained and tested separately. reports/canopy_naip_bakersfield.json holds the figures. A cell the urban boundary cuts keeps the USFS figure for its assessed part, since that part is normally where its homes are and the rest is open ground. In 23 ranked cells under half of the in-city homes (weighted by residents) are in the assessed part: newer housing inside the city limits but past the 2020 urban line. There the figure is for the whole cell, USFS canopy area plus modelled canopy for the rest (canopy_source usfs+naip-model, canopy_quality completed, coverage_frac still the USFS share). Completing all 217 partly assessed cells was tested and rejected: it left the top 25 unchanged but moved 97 cells by 100 ranks or more, most of them cells whose homes were already measured and whose unassessed part is empty land. The current build has 1,987 ranked residential cells, all with a tree-cover value: 1,751 from the USFS map (1,557 fully assessed, 194 partly), 23 partly assessed and completed, and 213 modelled. No cell uses the satellite greenness stand-in or the legacy height model any longer. canopy_source distinguishes usfs-2022, usfs+naip-model and naip-model; coverage_frac gives the assessed share. The tree-canopy layer renders missing canopy in gray; the separate greenness layer covers all cells.

Planning note 213 ranked cells past the USFS map carry modelled tree cover, labeled “modelled 2022” in cell details. Orchards count as tree cover there, as they do in the USFS map, though they shade no street. Along the edges of some crop fields the model still reads a thin strip as canopy; in cells that are all farmland this can add up to about 15 points, and such cells hold few or no residents. Planting scenarios show a whole-area canopy total only for fully assessed USFS cells. Percentages describe assessed cover, not individual trees or verified planting space.

Population

Estimated residents allocated into map cells from larger census areas.

Use it to

Bring the scale of resident need into investment decisions. Population helps distinguish a sparsely occupied canopy gap from an area where a similar gap affects the surroundings of many more residents.

Why this approach

ACS five-year data supplies population, age and income on a consistent small-area geography. In Bakersfield, each block group’s residents are placed where its homes are, using the city’s own building-footprint layer, so farmland, oil fields, industrial lots and empty desert inside the city limits carry no residents and cannot be ranked. Until November 11, 2026 residents were spread evenly over each block group’s land; that ranked 3,767 areas, 1,790 of which hold no housing.

Source & calculation details
ACS 2024 five-year estimates (2020–2024), at block-group level. Each block group’s residents are divided among the cells it overlaps in proportion to the weight of the residential buildings in each (dasymetric allocation), counting only buildings inside the city limits for a cell’s share and every building in the block group for the total, so residents outside the city are not assigned to edge cells. A building’s weight is its share, by footprint area, of the residents the 2020 Census counted in its census block (8,870 blocks in the box, from Census TIGERweb), so apartments, whose footprints understate the people in them, and mobile-home parks, whose small households footprint area overstates, are weighted as the Census found them. Homes built since April 2020 are not in that count: a block with more main residential footprints (80 m² or larger) than it had housing units in 2020 has grown by the difference, 15,239 homes in all, and each is credited with its block group’s 2020 residents per housing unit. The 2020 block counts set shares only; the totals are the 2020–2024 ACS block-group estimates. Footprints: City of Bakersfield GIS “Building General Structure Existing Site”, 228,012 footprints over the metropolitan general-plan area, last edited July 2026, fetched November 11, 2026. A footprint counts when its use type is RESIDENTIAL (187,929); 14,222 footprints with no use type yet, most digitised from the 2026 aerial survey, count when their centre lies in a residential designation of the Metropolitan Bakersfield General Plan land-use layer, so new subdivisions are included; commercial, industrial, government, agricultural and multi-tenant commercial footprints are left out. After a 30–4,000 m² size filter, 192,093 footprints remain. Checks, re-run on every build: 0.95 footprints per ACS housing unit across the 344 block groups touching the city; by block-group income quartile 0.84, 0.93, 0.97, 1.17 (the lowest is apartment block groups, where one building holds many homes), inside the pipeline’s 2× limit. The cells sum to 410,680 residents against the ACS figure of 411,986 for the city. Against 2020 Census block counts interpolated to the same cells, the old even spread correlates 0.86 (mean error 45 residents per cell) and footprint area alone, which uses no block counts, 0.93 (30); that second figure is the independent test of the footprint layer. The block-weighted placement used here reads 0.94 (26), which is expected rather than a test, since it uses those counts. In the city’s largest mobile-home park cell, footprint area alone placed 583 residents where the 2020 Census counted 305; the block-weighted placement gives 312. Two block groups with no mapped housing keep area weighting. Mobile-home parks are in the footprint layer (2,999 footprints in mobile-home zoning) and carry residents.

Planning note Cell populations are allocated estimates, not household counts. Within a census block, footprint area sets the shares, and storeys are not recorded. New homes are credited at their block group’s 2020 household size, which may not be theirs. People in group quarters without a residential footprint (a jail, a care home) are placed with their block group’s housing; blocks that had housing in 2020 but hold no residential footprint in the city layer pass their residents to the rest of their block group. Untyped new buildings in commercial designations, which may include new apartments, are left out until the city types them. Published ACS margins of error are carried into the likely-rank bands (see Rank stability), which hold heat, tree cover and walking time fixed and spread each block group’s or tract’s re-draw over cells by whole-cell overlap area, an approximation of the point model’s boundary-clipped, building-weighted allocation; the bands are sensitivity ranges under those assumptions, not confidence intervals.

Residents aged 65+

An estimated, smoothed share of older residents.

Use it to

Bring older residents into the shortlist for shade investment and outreach planning. The seniors preset lets staff see how prioritizing this population changes the areas they would investigate first.

Why this approach

CDC identifies adults aged 65+ as more susceptible to heat-related problems, supporting age as one input. The project smooths small-cell percentages to avoid unstable extremes; the 100-person smoothing strength is a design choice, not a CDC recommendation.

Source & calculation details
Start with ACS block-group population aged 65+, allocate to cells, then smooth: (estimated residents 65+ + 100 × citywide age-65+ fraction) ÷ (cell population + 100). The displayed percentage and score use this smoothed result.

Planning note Shares are smoothed area estimates. They do not identify individual residents or their health needs.

A/C access

Census estimates of occupied homes with air conditioning.

Use it to

Identify areas where limited home cooling adds a reason to investigate shade and public cooling access. This gives the investment discussion a housing dimension alongside canopy and population.

Why this approach

Census LACE combines AHS air-conditioning information with the ACS’s geographic coverage to estimate prevalence at tract scale. We chose it to use a documented, cooling-specific national product instead of treating local income rank as A/C availability.

Source & calculation details
US Census LACE 2023 AC_PE, joined by tract and aggregated using allocated occupied housing units from ACS 2024 B25003_001E. Each overlap contributes occupied homes × overlap fraction; A/C homes = that count × tract rate / 100. The cell rate is summed A/C homes ÷ summed occupied homes × 100. LACE is an experimental modeled product drawing on the American Housing Survey and ACS. The pipeline retains an income-rank fallback where estimates are unavailable; ac_src now explicitly records lace or income-model. ac_coverage records the share of allocated occupied homes with a valid LACE estimate; incomplete coverage does not become a zero-A/C observation. All 1,987 ranked residential cells are LACE-backed; 1 cell whose source block groups report no occupied homes use the population-weighted tract rate instead (ac_src = lace-pop). A/C prevalence is nearly uniform in Bakersfield (96.5–100% across ranked cells, median 99.8%), so it carries no default weight in the ranking; the “Homes without A/C” preset turns it on.

Planning note Experimental tract estimates, weighted by occupied homes. It does not establish working or affordable A/C in a particular home.

Walking access & potential cooling sites

Estimated walking time to a discovery site, with an official county directory alongside.

Use it to

Screen for areas where residents may face a long walk to a potential cooling facility. Use the map to focus a facility-verification exercise or a discussion about where additional relief may be needed.

Why this approach

OSM provides facility locations and a pedestrian graph in a common, reproducible source. Shortest-path routing represents mapped street connections more realistically than straight-line distance alone. This is a facility-access screen, not an emergency service directory.

Source & calculation details
The city file contains 39 mapped sites across library, community center, pool and senior/shelter categories. The walking pipeline uses an OSM pedestrian graph, multi-source shortest paths, straight-line snap distances at both ends, and 4.8 km/h. Revised September 9, 2026: streets tagged access=private (gated and private subdivisions) are now part of the graph, because residents can walk out of their own subdivision, and each cell center and each site is joined to its eight nearest network nodes with the best door-to-door total kept, so a point no longer snaps to an isolated footpath when a street is a few meters further. A cell whose only nearby network is a fragment (fewer than 40 nodes within 2 km, such as farm tracks) is treated as off-network and shows the straight-line estimate, labeled. At that revision, on the 3,767 areas ranked at the time, 1,142 residential cells got a shorter estimate (median 68 → 61 min), 45 cells the network does not reach show a straight-line estimate instead and say so, and 62 cells carry a detour-review flag because the route is more than three times the straight line (a barrier, or a remaining map gap). The shipped model puts no weight on walking time, so no score or rank changed. Of the 4,054 cells in the current overlay CSV, 4,005 are routed and 49 use the straight-line fallback (0 of them ranked residential areas); 1,341 ranked areas carry an approach-review flag because the cell-to-network connection exceeds 100 m.

Planning note Verify designation, hours, fees and accessibility. Category filters change markers only; walking estimates remain based on all mapped sites.

The directory is not an open-now feed; the county page shows today’s status. No mapped discovery site is currently excluded for lacking A/C. access_snap_m records the straight-line cell-to-network connection; connections over 100 m are flagged for approach review, without implying that shorter approaches are verified. Scenarios

Street planting share

Choose how much of the theoretical mapped street capacity to explore.

Use it to

Translate a priority area into a concrete program-scale question: what would planting a quarter, half or all of the mapped street capacity involve? Compare tree counts, canopy gain and indicative cost before commissioning site surveys.

Why this approach

Street length connects the scenario to a physical intervention that a public-works team can investigate. Both sides at 10 m spacing gives a transparent, repeatable capacity assumption. It is a project scenario convention, not a verified spacing requirement for every street.

Source & calculation details
Capacity = floor(mapped eligible street centerline meters × 2 ÷ 10), counting only street inside the study boundary, so an edge cell cannot offer streets outside the study area as capacity. Trees = round(capacity × selected share / 100), capped by cell area minus known aerial canopy area, divided by 40 m². With a usable whole-cell baseline, its remaining-canopy ceiling also applies. Surviving trees = round(trees × survival share), with the share editable beside the cost (default 70%). Added canopy percentage points = surviving trees × 40 ÷ cell area × 100; cost counts every planted tree. A change from 10% to 15% canopy is +5 percentage points, not a 5% relative increase. Scenarios require mapped street capacity, not 99% accuracy. Tree counts, cost, added canopy and illustrative air cooling are conditional on feasible planting and new, non-overlapping crowns. The remaining-area ceiling subtracts known aerial canopy without extrapolating partial coverage over unassessed land. A whole-cell current-to-future canopy total is shown only with at least 99% aerial coverage; otherwise only added canopy is shown. The slider starts at 25%; zero means no planting. Choices persist during unit and weight changes for this session.

Planning note Capacity is theoretical; confirm ownership, existing trees, utilities and space. Partial and unknown canopy coverage allow conditional scenarios; they do not establish a reliable future total or verified planting capacity.

Trees, maturity & cost

An indicative scale of investment; no financial return is calculated.

Use it to

Put an initial scale of investment beside the shortlist. A shared unit-cost and crown-area assumption lets staff compare scenarios consistently and prepare questions for arborists and procurement teams.

Why this approach

A linear model makes every dollar and canopy increment traceable to the tree count. The cost per tree is a band, not a point: no Bakersfield contract price is published online, so the app offers three sourced presets and lets you type your own. The 40 m² mature crown is a planning assumption.

Source & calculation details
Scenario cost = planted trees × the cost per tree, planting-side only; pruning after establishment, sidewalk repair and replacement are not costed. Three presets, from published California figures (September 2026): Low $500, planting only by city crews with no separate establishment budget, which is Bakersfield’s own estimate (its $500,000 budget funds “roughly 1,000 trees”); Base $2,000, planting plus about a year of establishment care, or a grant program’s all-in average (Concord’s USDA grant: $1 million for 500 trees including a plan and workforce); High $3,500, planting plus three years of watering and program overhead (San Francisco’s 3500 Trees project: $12 million for 3,500 street trees; Los Angeles Public Works reported $4,351 per tree with five years of watering in 2022). The default is the low band so older briefings and scenario files reproduce; the number you set is written into every export. Assume 40 m² of additional mature crown per tree. Canopy and cooling are credited only to the share of planted trees assumed to reach maturity (default 70%, editable; scenario files record it). That default is a planning placeholder: the US Forest Service’s monitoring of the Sacramento Shade program found 42.4% of trees alive 22 years after planting (Ko, Lee, McPherson & Roman 2015), and Berkeley and Oakland cohorts lost 34% in two years, so a program without a watering budget should test lower values. Crown area is an assumption, not a detected tree count; additive canopy assumes no overlap.

Planning note Indicative cost at assumed maturity; not a bid, lifecycle budget or financial ROI. Growth and crown overlap are not modeled; survival is an assumed share (editable beside the cost, default 70%), not a predicted outcome.

Illustrative air cooling

A literature-based scale estimate of possible cooling at maturity.

Use it to

Connect tree investment to cooler streets and shade where people walk and wait. Added crowns can reduce direct sun and surface heating; the air-cooling number describes a different, area-wide effect and does not capture the full benefit of standing in shade.

Why this approach

A review of urban cooling simulations estimates about 0.3 °C of afternoon air cooling per 10 percentage points of canopy under clear-sky summer conditions. We use that relationship for an illustrative scenario, separate from the satellite surface-temperature layer.

Source & calculation details
Apply 0.3 °C air-temperature reduction per 10 percentage points of added canopy: cooling = canopy gain ÷ 10 × 0.3. Krayenhoff et al. (2021) synthesize higher-quality numerical modeling studies at this approximate scale. The coefficient describes clear-sky summer afternoons, not annual average cooling or a calibrated local response. Fahrenheit differences multiply by 1.8, without adding 32. This is air cooling, not surface cooling. For example, +5 canopy percentage points gives an illustrative 0.15 °C air reduction. Research comparing tree-covered surfaces with built surfaces does not provide a transferable per-tree forecast for this cell; no surface-temperature change is currently modeled.

Planning note Literature-based illustration at maturity, not a locally calibrated forecast or a count of residents cooled.

Cells, names & unranked land

Hexagons are consistent analysis units, not census blocks or neighborhood boundaries.

Use it to

Compare the same geographic units across all map layers. A consistent grid helps staff move from a city overview to an identifiable area for follow-up without changing the unit of analysis.

Why this approach

H3 cells give each area a stable identifier and allow different source geographies to be combined. Keeping developed and undeveloped cells visible preserves geographic context while residential-only ranking keeps the shortlist aligned with resident priorities.

Source & calculation details
H3 resolution 9, about 0.1 km² per cell; exact geodesic areas are stored per cell. Current build: 1,987 residential, 586 developed without residents and 1,481 undeveloped cells, totaling 4,054. Residential means estimated population ≥ 1. Development uses OSM street-length/building thresholds. Each area is named by cross streets from the City of Bakersfield’s street centreline layer (pipeline step 04i): the intersection inside the area, preferring arterials and collectors over local streets and then the one nearest the centre (“Ming Ave & Stine Rd”); an area with no intersection inside it is named by direction from the nearest one (“N of Akers Rd & Planz Rd”), with the distance once that passes a quarter mile. Under the name, the detail panel gives the nearest intersection of two through streets (“0.3 mi SW of Ming Ave & Stine Rd”), and the search box matches either line. 2,164 of 4,054 areas (1,804 of the 1,987 ranked) hold an intersection; every name is unique. An intersection is where at least three segment ends of two differently named streets meet, so a street changing its name is not one; ramps, freeways and unbuilt streets are left out. Checked against the city’s separate intersection layer, 1,782 of the 1,804 ranked areas named by an inside intersection have that same intersection there; most of the other 22 are streets newer than that layer. Until November 11, 2026 areas were named after the nearest OpenStreetMap place, which gave names such as “Village at Lakeside E 21”. Each cell also carries the city or census-designated community that covers most of its land inside the study boundary (Census TIGERweb place polygons, step 04d); the list’s “Show” menu limits the list and fades the map to one of them, and exports carry it as city_or_community. A cell on a city line is attributed to the side holding more of its in-boundary land, at the precision of the cell itself; cells on the study-area edge are labelled by the part inside it, the same part whose residents are counted.

Planning note Cells are not census blocks or neighborhood boundaries. A name locates an area; it is not a neighborhood boundary, and the streets named may be local ones, which is why the nearest major intersection is given too. Gray means unranked.

A canopy-exposure figure from the current build, stated with its limits.

Use it to

Frame an equity discussion using canopy and resident context, then inspect the candidate areas directly. The figure below is recomputed from the current build; it is an exposure count, not an equity comparison.

Why this approach

The figure counts estimated residents by the canopy of the cell they live in, independently of the composite score. It uses no income data, so it does not by itself establish an equity gap.

Source & calculation details

Planning note The exposure count mixes the aerial map and, for 213 fringe cells, a model of it; recheck on aerial-only cells before quoting it. No income-equity claim is made for Bakersfield in this build.

SB 535 disadvantaged communities

Census tracts designated by CalEPA under SB 535 (final designation, September 2026).

Use it to

See which priority areas also fall inside a state-designated disadvantaged community, and rank within that set. Bakersfield’s planting program commits at least 80% of new trees to designated tracts; the “Disadvantaged communities” preset asks where, within that commitment, heat and missing shade are worst.

Why this approach

SB 535 is the designation state and local programs already screen against, so the map carries it as published rather than a locally invented equity index. The 2026 designation qualifies a tract under any of four criteria: the top 25% of CalEnviroScreen 5.0 overall scores, the top 5% of CES 5.0 pollution burden, at least 25% overlap with a 2022 (CES 4.0) designated tract, or land under the control of a federally recognized tribe. It is a funding-eligibility designation, not a measurement of heat or shade, which is why it carries no weight in the recommended mix and leads its own preset instead.

Source & calculation details
CalEPA ArcGIS feature service SB_535_Disadvantaged_Communities_2026 (tract layer plus the three tribal-boundary layers), queried for the study bounding box on November 2, 2026 and cached as sb535_bakersfield.geojson: 92 Kern County tracts touch the box, 68 under CES 5.0 top 25% and 24 as CES 4.0 carry-over tracts; no tribal land. Each cell’s value is the share of its area inside any designated polygon (0–1, the union, so stacked designations never exceed 1), with the criterion and tract GEOID of the polygon covering most of the cell. 1,494 of 4,054 cells are at least half inside a designated tract and 1,624 touch one. The share enters the score on a fixed 0–1 scale (no percentile clip), so a wholly designated cell reads 1 regardless of the other cells. With the recommended weights the term is zero and no rank changed when it was added. Exported as sb535_dac_share_pct, sb535_dac_criterion and sb535_dac_tract.

Planning note The designation applies to whole census tracts; a cell straddling a tract boundary reads a partial share. Eligibility rules for a specific grant program should be checked against the program’s own guidelines and the official CalEPA map.

Priority populations

Disadvantaged and low-income communities that count toward state climate-investment targets (CARB, 2026).

Use it to

Report planting against grant terms. State climate money, including CAL FIRE’s urban forestry grants, has to benefit “priority populations”: the SB 535 disadvantaged communities plus the low-income communities and households AB 1550 added. The layer shows which areas fall inside one, and each ward summary gives the share of residents who do.

Why this approach

SB 535 alone misses the low-income tracts. In Bakersfield, 38% of residents in ranked areas live in an SB 535 community and 51% in a priority population area; the difference is 203 ranked areas with about 51,000 residents in low-income communities that are not SB 535 tracts. The map carries CARB’s own layer rather than recomputing income thresholds. It is a funding designation, not a measurement of heat or shade: it has no weight, no preset and no effect on any rank.

Source & calculation details
California Air Resources Board, Priority Populations 5.0, in effect for projects selected on or after September 18, 2026 (CalEnviroScreen 5.0, the final 2026 SB 535 designation, and AB 1550 low-income communities). Queried for the study bounding box on November 11, 2026 and cached as priority_pop_bakersfield.geojson: 207 polygons touch the box, 119 of them priority population areas. Each polygon carries one designation: disadvantaged community; disadvantaged and low-income community; low-income community; or low-income community within ½ mile of a disadvantaged community. Polygons CARB marks “not a priority population area”, including the half-mile buffer where the tract is not low-income, are not counted. Each cell’s value is the share of its area inside any counted polygon (0–1), with the designation covering most of the cell. 1,759 of 4,054 cells are at least half inside one: 1,199 disadvantaged and low-income, 297 disadvantaged only, 141 low-income, 122 low-income within the half-mile buffer. Every cell’s share is at least its SB 535 share (checked on every build), so the two layers agree. Exported as priority_population_share_pct and priority_population_designation.

Planning note Low-income households qualify wherever they live; that is a property of a household, not a place, and no map shows it. Whether a specific project counts as benefiting a priority population depends on CARB’s benefit criteria for the project type, not on location alone.

Sorting, filtering and searching the list

Sort re-orders the Priority areas list by one measure; rank badges and the map never change.

Use it to

Read the same ranked list from a different angle: the most people first, the hottest ground first, the least tree cover first, the longest walk first. Filters narrow the list to areas that meet a threshold; search finds one area by name or cell number.

Why this approach

The rank badge is the answer to the question set under What matters most, so it stays fixed. Sorting is a reading aid, which is why the count line says what the list is sorted by and in which direction whenever it is not in rank order.

Source & calculation details
Sort options. Need: the priority score under the current mix, the same order as the rank badges. Residents: estimated people living in the area (ACS, allocated into the hexagon). Surface heat: summer late-morning land-surface temperature from Landsat (about 10:30 am overpass). Tree cover: share of assessed land under tree crowns (or the greenness index where no aerial assessment exists). Walk to cooling: estimated minutes on foot to the nearest mapped cool place along the pedestrian network. Age 65+: estimated share of residents aged 65 or older. Direction. “Most first” puts the largest value at the top; “least first” the smallest. Tree cover opens least first, every other measure most first, and either can be flipped. Filters. Walk over 15 minutes; 500 or more residents; tree cover under 10%; residents aged 65+ at 20% or more; plantable (mapped street capacity exists); full aerial tree data (drops modelled and partly assessed areas); stable top tier (in the best 10% in at least 80% of rank-stability re-draws). Filters combine, and the count line says “matching” instead of “ranked” while any is on. Areas with no value for the chosen measure sort as zero.

Planning note A sort is not a ranking. Use What matters most to change what the score rewards; use sort and filters to find areas within the ranking you already have.

Export & reload a scenario

Take the ranked list or a single selected area into a meeting as a file that records exactly what produced it, and load it back later.

Use it to

Share a shortlist or one area with colleagues, keep a record of the weights and planting share behind a recommendation, and reopen the same view. A CSV opens in a spreadsheet; a JSON file reloads into this map. Nothing is uploaded: files are generated in the browser and saved on your computer.

Ranked list — the Export & share section

Save scenario (JSON) saves the ranked list with the full scenario header, for reopening. All ranked areas (CSV) saves every ranked cell in the order the current weights put them, one row per cell, with plain column names (see the column key below). Selected area — in the area panel

Why this approach

A ranking only means something with its weights, assumptions and data version attached, so every export carries them: the weights in force, the population multiplier, the preset if one matches, the planting constants, per-cell source and coverage flags, and the SHA-256 fingerprint of the data file that was loaded. Source & calculation details
rank/score are computed live from the exported weights; rank_recommended_mix/score_recommended_mix are the pipeline defaults shipped with the data. Vegetation greenness & supporting fields

Explore satellite greenness separately from tree canopy.

Use it to

Compare living vegetation with tree cover to identify areas worth investigating for shade. A greener area can contain grass or shrubs and still need trees; the two layers help staff ask that question without confusing greenness with canopy.

Why this approach

Satellite greenness supplies broad vegetation context across the grid, including outside aerial canopy coverage. It is publicly available in Explore, leaving the default priority workflow focused. The active planting calculator uses mapped street length, not the buffered right-of-way area fields.

Source & calculation details
The greenness display rescales the legacy veg field from 0–45 to an index of 0–100. The source linearly maps clamped Sentinel-2 NDVI 0.05–0.65 to 0–45; this is an index, not a measured vegetation fraction. Subtracting canopy from it does not estimate lawn or non-tree cover; row_m2 and row_canopy describe buffered street areas in the canopy pipeline. Current planting scenarios use street_m and whole-cell area, not row_m2. canopy_m2 is pixel-derived area for usfs-2022, and modelled percentage × cell area for naip-model. See canopy_source and canopy_quality before combining sources.

Planning note Greenness does not establish percent cover or planting feasibility; exported street-buffer fields do not establish ownership or plantability.

By ward

The ranking rolled up by ward, with the starred areas’ planting totals.

Use it to

Answer “how many trees are we planting in Ward 5, what does it cost, and how many residents does it reach” in one row, under whatever priorities are in force and with the areas you starred.

Why this approach

Bakersfield publishes its seven council wards on the city GIS service; the app rolls the ranking up by ward and can outline them on the map. Street room

How many trees the mapped streets in an area could hold, drawn as a layer and read as a word on every row.

Use it to

See need and feasibility together. An area that needs shade and has plenty of street room is an early win; one that needs shade and has little room needs parks, schoolyards or private planting instead, and should be planned differently rather than dropped.

Why this approach

Street room is the number of tree spots that the mapped street centrelines inside the study boundary could hold at 10 m spacing on both sides. It is the same ceiling the planting scenario starts from, shown directly. Streets come from OpenStreetMap; utilities, driveways, sidewalk width and existing trees are not known, so it is an upper bound, not a surveyed count. The words “little”, “some” and “plenty” are thirds of this study area’s ranked areas that have any mapped street; “no mapped street” means none inside the boundary. On the map, deeper colour means less room, keeping the rule that deeper always means more need.

Planning note Street room is a planning screen for where to look first, not a count of plantable sites. A city’s own right-of-way, utility and sidewalk layers turn it into a planting plan; ask public works for them.
